# Proof Narrative: Adult neurogenesis occurs in the human neocortex.

## Verdict

**Verdict: DISPROVED**

The evidence is clear and settled: the adult human brain does not grow new neurons in the neocortex. Three independent studies reached the same conclusion using different methods, and no credible evidence has emerged to challenge them.

## What was claimed?

The claim is that the mature human brain — specifically the outer layers of the cerebral cortex responsible for thought, perception, and language — continuously produces new neurons throughout adulthood. This idea matters because it would have significant implications for brain plasticity, recovery from injury, and our understanding of cognitive aging. If true, it might suggest the brain can replenish cells lost to disease or time.

## What did we find?

The most direct test of this claim used an ingenious technique borrowed from nuclear history. Atmospheric nuclear weapons testing in the mid-20th century released a pulse of radioactive carbon-14 that was absorbed into DNA worldwide at the moment cells divided. By measuring the carbon-14 content of neurons in human tissue, scientists could determine precisely when those cells were born. When researchers applied this method to the neocortex — the large, layered sheet of cortical tissue covering the brain — they found that every neuron tested had been born around the time of the individual's birth or shortly after. None showed carbon-14 signatures consistent with formation in adulthood.

In the same study, patients who had received a chemical marker for cell division as part of cancer treatment provided another window into this question. Over five hundred cells in the neocortex had absorbed this marker, showing that some form of cell activity was occurring — but not a single one of those cells turned out to be a neuron. They were all non-neuronal support cells.

An independent research team tackled the question separately, in non-human primates, using a different laboratory technique. Their search for new neurons in the neocortex came up empty as well. Where they did find new neurons — confirming their method worked — was in the hippocampus and olfactory bulb, brain regions anatomically and functionally distinct from the neocortex. The neocortex showed cell proliferation, but none of it produced neurons.

A third independent evaluation systematically reviewed the full body of evidence and concluded that the scientific basis for claiming adult neocortical neurogenesis simply does not hold up. This review, published in one of the field's leading journals, examined the same claims and found them unsupported.

Earlier reports in the late 1990s had suggested that macaque monkeys might grow new neurons in their prefrontal and temporal cortex. Those findings generated real excitement at the time, but they relied on a labeling technique that can inadvertently mark cells undergoing DNA repair or dying — not just dividing. The follow-up studies described above used more rigorous methods and could not replicate the original results. The field now regards those early positive reports as methodological artifacts.

## What should you keep in mind?

This verdict applies specifically to the neocortex — the part of the brain most people have in mind when thinking about higher cognition. It does not settle the question for other brain regions. There is an ongoing, active scientific debate about whether the adult human hippocampus generates new neurons, particularly in a region called the dentate gyrus. That debate remains genuinely unresolved as of 2024, with high-profile studies on both sides. The neocortical question, however, is not part of that controversy — researchers on both sides of the hippocampal debate treat the neocortex as separately settled.

It is also worth noting that "no new neurons" does not mean the adult brain is static. Existing neurons form new connections constantly, and non-neuronal cells do proliferate in the adult cortex. The finding is specifically that the neocortex does not add to its neuron count after early development.

## How was this verified?

This claim was evaluated by identifying published peer-reviewed studies that directly tested for adult neocortical neurogenesis in humans or closely related primates, verifying that each study explicitly rejected the claim, and checking whether any subsequent research had overturned the consensus. The process included a deliberate search for counter-evidence, including re-examining the original positive reports and scanning for more recent work through 2023.
